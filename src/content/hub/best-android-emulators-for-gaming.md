Ask which Android emulator is best and the honest answer is another question: best for which game? The right pick depends on what you play, what your PC can handle and whether the publisher tolerates emulators at all. Below are the main options, what each one needs, and a ten-minute test you can run on your own machine before you commit.

## Check for an official PC version first

Before installing any emulator, look for a way to play that the publisher supports directly. Many big mobile games now have one:

- **Native PC clients**: Genshin Impact, Honkai: Star Rail, Zenless Zone Zero, Wuthering Waves and Albion Online all have their own Windows versions. They run a proper PC build instead of the phone build inside a virtual Android device, so they usually look and run better.
- **Google Play Games on PC**: Google's own app runs a curated catalogue of Android games on Windows. Supercell put Clash of Clans and Clash Royale on it, which makes it the approved PC route for those two.
- **Publisher emulators**: Tencent's GameLoop is the emulator PUBG Mobile points players to for PC.

If your game has one of these, start there. You get support from the publisher, and you avoid the account questions that come with third-party emulators.

## What each emulator needs from your PC

Published minimums are low. They tell you what will start, which is a long way from what plays well, so treat them as the floor.

- **Google Play Games on PC**: Windows 10 (v2004) or newer, a Windows admin account, hardware virtualization turned on, and Windows Hypervisor Platform switched on during setup. Google also asks for the SSD to be the main drive.
- **BlueStacks 5**: the minimum is Windows 7 or newer, an Intel or AMD processor, 4 GB of RAM, 5 GB of free disk space, admin rights and current graphics drivers. BlueStacks recommends Windows 10 or newer, a processor with a single-thread benchmark score above 1000, a graphics chip scoring 750 or more, and virtualization turned on.
- **MuMuPlayer (Windows)**: Windows 7 or newer with DirectX 11.0 or OpenGL 4.x, 4 GB of RAM or more, graphics at GTX 950 level or above, at least 2 GB free on the installation drive and 1.5 GB free on the system drive.

In practice, the parts that make the biggest difference are the same for every emulator: virtualization turned on in the BIOS, the emulator and its games on an SSD, at least 8 GB of RAM if you want to run anything else alongside it, and current graphics drivers.

## Which emulator for which kind of game

### Gacha RPGs and turn-based games

These are forgiving. Any of the general emulators will run them, and multi-instance support helps if you play several accounts or several games. If the game has an official PC client, use that instead, because you get proper PC graphics settings and the publisher's support.

### Mobile MMORPGs

Long sessions and auto-play make stability the priority. The emulator that is still steady after an hour beats the one with the highest frame rate in the first minute. Multi-instance tools matter if you run alts, but read the game's rules on multiple accounts first.

### Competitive shooters

Follow the publisher. For PUBG Mobile that means GameLoop. Call of Duty: Mobile is a different case: Activision does not support emulators, even though GameLoop lists the game. In PUBG Mobile, emulator players are placed in lobbies with other emulator players, and a mobile player who joins a squad with an emulator player is pulled into that lobby too.

### Supercell games

Use Google Play Games on PC for Clash of Clans and Clash Royale, since that is the route Supercell announced.

### Mac players

On Apple Silicon Macs, the main options are MuMuPlayer's Mac app and BlueStacks Air. Both are separate products from their Windows versions, so check their own requirements and pricing.

## How to test two emulators fairly

Torn between two emulators? Ten minutes of testing tells you more than any review:

1. Install the same game on both, using the same account region.
2. Match the settings: same resolution, same graphics preset in the game, same frame-rate cap in the emulator.
3. Pick a repeatable scene, such as a training area, a quiet town or a fixed stage.
4. Let the game warm up for a few minutes, then play for ten. Watch for stutters, crashes, input lag and how loud your fans get.
5. Check CPU, GPU and RAM use in Windows Task Manager while you play.

A steady 60 FPS feels better than a frame rate that jumps between 60 and 120. Do not judge an emulator from a loading screen or an uncapped menu.

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Installing several emulators at once.** They compete for virtualization and can conflict with each other. Uninstall the one you are not using.
- **Leaving virtualization off.** Every emulator in this guide either needs it or runs far worse without it. See our guide to turning it on.
- **Downloading from mirror sites.** Get emulators only from their official websites, and games only from the store inside the emulator.
- **Skipping account binding.** Link your game account to Google, Apple, Facebook or the publisher's own login before you move between phone and PC, so a broken emulator install cannot take your progress with it.

## Which one should you pick?

Use the game's official PC route whenever one exists. If there is none and the publisher allows emulators, start with BlueStacks 5 on Windows or MuMuPlayer on Windows or Mac, set them up carefully, and only switch if your game runs badly after the settings in our performance guide. For PUBG Mobile, use GameLoop, and for Call of Duty: Mobile, play on a supported phone or tablet.
